<SummarySection title="Summary">

Emisys is a digital platform for employee funds, the savings and credit cooperatives common in Colombia. It lets a fund administer, operate and control the full lifecycle of the credit-card products it offers to its members, in a way that is secure, traceable and open to third parties.

It is a work project, not a personal one: it is being built at Tres Astronautas for a client. Emisys is one half of a two-part product. This half is the visual layer and the channel of communication with the funds, while Mottika, documented as its own project, is the transactional engine behind it.

The platform has three web apps: a back-office for the operator, a portal for each fund and an app for each fund's members, called associates.

</SummarySection>

<ObjectivesSection title="Goals">

The planning work started from an impact map that links business goals to the people who use the system and to what they need. Its five business objectives are:

- **Faster onboarding and card administration.** Reduce the time from registering a member to having an active card, and the share of requests that need rework.
- **Continuity and reliability.** Keep the flow of operations running even when a third party fails, with safe retries and recovery.
- **Financial control and reconciliation.** Shorten reconciliation and resolve differences inside the cycle.
- **Governed interoperability.** Connect with third parties through stable, versioned contracts.
- **Service, compliance and auditability.** Leave a trace of every relevant operation.

Targets for these goals were deliberately left open until a baseline can be measured after go-live.

</ObjectivesSection>

<FeaturesSection title="Features">

The impact map describes five business actors and seven external parties. The external parties, such as a card processor or an accounting system, are modeled only as integration points and never as internal domains.

| Actor | What they can do |
|---|---|
| Fund administrator | Register and update associates, link them to card products, assign cards from batches, activate, block and replace cards, and follow claims on transactions. |
| Associate | See their card, balance and movements, block the card or manage the PIN with stronger identity checks, and open a claim from a specific transaction. |
| Operator in the back-office | Configure funds, limits and a versioned product catalog with approval, manage card batches, and watch an operations dashboard with retries. |
| Reconciliation and accounting analyst | Reconcile periods, manage differences, validate charges and send accounting movements, with period close blocked while exceptions remain. |

### How it works

Emisys is the orchestrator and system of record of a card program run by an employee fund. It is not the switch, the credit authorizer, the reconciliation engine or the ledger, and it never moves money: it calculates, requests, records, evidences and controls. The life of a card goes like this:

1. **Onboarding the fund.** An operator sets up a fund through a checklist with evidence, and the fund goes live only when every mandatory item is met. Products are defined once and then enabled per fund, with overrides.
2. **Registering members.** Associates arrive from the fund's own systems or in bulk, into one canonical model. The fund remains the authority on credit decisions.
3. **Supply.** The fund requests cards, which are grouped into batches and sent to the card processor and manufacturer. Cards return as inventory.
4. **Assignment and delivery.** The fund assigns an available card to a member, which consumes inventory exactly once, and records the physical delivery.
5. **Activation.** The member activates the card in their own app: confirm they hold it, pass a second factor and set the PIN in a secure keypad owned by the processor. Emisys never sees or stores the PIN.
6. **Authorization.** When a purchase arrives, the switch applies its first filter, and Emisys adds context and routes the request to the fund's authorizer within a tight deadline. A timeout is recorded as indeterminate, never as approve or reject.
7. **Transactions and corrections.** Every card operation is one record that accumulates facts: authorization, capture, settlement. Reversals, voids, refunds and chargebacks stay separate and are never merged.
8. **Money control and close.** For each fund and period, Emisys computes the funding and collateral required and records evidence and approvals. It then sends the period's sources to an external reconciliation engine, rates consumption, validates charges, issues the invoice and maps the result to accounting movements.

### How it communicates

Emisys talks to Mottika and to third parties only through versioned, contract-first REST APIs and events. It never reads another system's database. Events are published with an outbox and delivered at least once, and receivers are idempotent. Every operation carries a correlation identifier and a controlled state, and the interface never shows data it does not have: when information from a third party is not available, it says so.

### Status in the code

Identity and access, funds, products, associates and rules are built, together with the web shells with login, password reset, theming, languages and installable PWAs. Card batches and lifecycle, transactions, statements, reconciliation and billing are specified in contracts and documentation and are being built in later chunks.

<MotivationSection title="Why the product was built">

Card operations at employee funds often run on scattered manual processes: emails, loose files and data typed twice. They are slow, opaque and prone to error, and they make reconciliation and audits harder than they should be.

Emisys replaces that with a single platform where each actor has the tools for their part of the lifecycle, and where the connections with processors, manufacturers and accounting systems are explicit and governed.

</MotivationSection>

<ImplementationSection title="How it was built">

### Architecture

- **Two halves in one monorepo:** a Go backend and a web workspace. The architecture follows a C4 design reviewed by an external architecture partner at fixed checkpoints, and changes to it are requested rather than made unilaterally.
- **Hexagonal backend sliced by feature.** Services hold modules per feature and share an internal platform library for configuration, HTTP and gRPC servers, logging, security and safe SQL.
- **Authentication and authorization are separate.** Identity is delegated to an OpenID Connect provider, and a separate authorization service answers every permission check over gRPC. If it is unreachable, the answer is a service error and never a silent denial.
- **An API gateway** routes requests by path prefix to the right service.

### Domain model

The domain is large, and the core of it can be read in a few ideas:

- **Fund** is the root of isolation. Nothing is visible across funds. It moves from draft to active and can be suspended and reactivated.
- **Associate** belongs to one fund and links to the products enabled for it.
- **Product** is versioned: editable, authorized, published and suspended, with immutable codes and a change history.
- **Card** stores only a masked number. Its states are assigned, delivered, active and temporarily blocked, with cancelled as a permanent end. Each block has its own origin, so lifting one never lifts another.
- **Authorization operation and transaction** are separate: the first is the request-response moment, the second accumulates the facts of the purchase over time.
- **Settlement, reconciliation and billing** are periods and runs with versioned results, so each figure can be explained later.
- **Cross-cutting concepts** are custom roles by scope, audit records, documents as evidence and integration events.

A few invariants hold everywhere: every critical operation is idempotent, a technical state is never shown as a business state, and no fund can see another's data.

### Quality attributes

The quality attributes were derived from principles and traced to their sources. The ones that matter most, and how they are met:

- **Availability.** The authorization path gets the highest tier, with active-passive cores, multi-zone data, zero-downtime deploys and rebuild drills.
- **Latency.** Authorization is synchronous with a tight budget and no queues on that path, while long jobs are asynchronous with visible progress.
- **Isolation and scale.** Rate limits per fund so that a noisy fund cannot hurt others.
- **Security.** Second factor for every person, deny-by-default access, fund context resolved on the server, encryption in transit and at rest, and no card number, security code or PIN stored or logged.
- **Resilience.** Timeouts and circuit breakers per third party, retries only for transient faults, and an unreachable authority always means access is denied.
- **Auditability.** Every effect, and every denial, is logged so that a critical transaction can be rebuilt end to end.
- **Interoperability.** Versioned contracts in one catalog, invalid messages rejected at the edge, and idempotency at every boundary.

### UX design

The blueprint defines four surfaces: the back-office, the fund portal, the member app and a governance portal where approvers review settlement and collateral. The member app is phone-first, with one task per screen. The key journeys are:

- A member activates a card and sets a PIN.
- A fund onboards members in bulk and gets a result per row.
- A fund requests a batch, receives it and assigns cards.
- A member or fund blocks, cancels or reissues a card.
- An operator handles degraded authorizations with only the permitted recovery actions.
- An analyst reconciles a period and approves billing, with four-eyes approval where it matters.

Its principles are one shared design system, the fund context always visible, four kinds of message (success, business rejection, technical failure and "pending confirmation") and sensitive data shown only through short-lived protected components.

### Contracts first

- **OpenAPI is the specification,** linted automatically, with one contract per capability. Contracts are delivered ahead of each build chunk, and a test fails when a contract operation has no handler.

### The web apps

- **Three apps by audience:** back-office, funds and associates, sharing a design system of shadcn-based primitives with theme tokens.
- **White-label by fund.** The funds and associates apps load each fund's branding after login.
- **Space Grotesk and Satoshi,** light and dark themes, Spanish and English, and installable PWAs with an offline route.
- **Server-side auth routes** in each app handle login, callback, logout, password reset and one-time codes.

### Delivery

- **Twenty weeks in five chunks,** from operational foundation to reconciliation and billing, with a short refinement and design phase up front and a stabilization phase at the end.
- **Security in CI.** Secret scanning over the full history, static analysis with coverage, and vulnerability scans of the source and of every container image, plus separate deploy workflows for each service and app.
- **Quality strategy.** Specs are reviewed before they are built, and testing goes deepest where risk is highest: authorization and its corrections, accounting duplication, isolation between funds and the PIN never being persisted. Integrations are tested at the contract level, and each defect links back to its spec.
- **Agent-assisted development.** The repository includes maps and rules for AI coding agents, with session folders and reviewer checklists, while people keep control of git.

</ImplementationSection>

<DecisionsSection title="Why it was done this way">

| Decision | Why | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| External parties as integration points | Emisys stays independent of each third party's internals. | Every integration needs a contract and an adapter. |
| Contract-first APIs | Teams can work in parallel against stable interfaces. | Contracts must be ready before building starts. |
| Separate authentication and authorization | Identity can change without touching business rules. | One more service to run and call. |
| Outbox, idempotency and at-least-once delivery | A failure never loses or duplicates an operation. | Receivers must be written to tolerate repeats. |
| Never show invented data | Users can trust what they see in a financial product. | Some screens show "unavailable" until a third party answers. |
| Three apps by audience | Each audience gets a focused experience and its own release. | Shared pieces live in a design system, and each app keeps its own types on purpose. |

</DecisionsSection>

<LimitationsSection title="Limitations and open items">

- **Work in progress.** Later chunks, such as transactions, statements, reconciliation and billing, are specified but not yet in code.
- **Third-party integration is pending.** The card-processor integration does not exist yet, and several details are still to be confirmed with that vendor.
- **Business targets are open** until a baseline is measured after go-live.
- **Dependencies on other parties** such as sandboxes, vendor contracts, legal sign-off and reconciliation formats sit on the critical path.
- **A contract check has a known gap.** The linter confirms that query parameters are declared, but not that handlers honor them, so extra tests cover that.
- **Some code is duplicated on purpose,** such as the query-parameter handler, until it is consolidated.

</LimitationsSection>
