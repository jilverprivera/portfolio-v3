<SummarySection title="Summary">

Emisys is a digital platform for employee funds, the savings and credit cooperatives common in Colombia. It lets a fund administer, operate and control the full lifecycle of the credit-card products it offers to its members, in a way that is secure, traceable and open to third parties.

It is a work project, not a personal one: it is being built at Tres Astronautas for a client. Emisys is one half of a two-part product. This half is the visual layer and the channel of communication with the funds, while Mottika, documented as its own project, is the transactional engine behind it.

The platform has three web apps: a back-office for the operator, a portal for each fund and an app for each fund's members, called associates.

</SummarySection>

<ChallengeSection title="Challenge & strategy">

### Challenge

Card operations at employee funds often ran on scattered manual processes: emails, loose files and data typed twice. They were slow, opaque and prone to error, and they made reconciliation and audits harder than they should have been. Each connection with a processor, a manufacturer or an accounting system added more hand-offs where something could be lost.

### Strategy

The planning work started from an impact map that linked business goals to the people who use the system and to what they need. Five objectives came out of it: faster onboarding and card administration, with less time from registering a member to having an active card and fewer requests needing rework; continuity and reliability, so operations keep flowing when a third party fails, with safe retries and recovery; financial control and reconciliation, shortening reconciliation and resolving differences inside the cycle; governed interoperability, connecting with third parties through stable, versioned contracts; and service, compliance and auditability, leaving a trace of every relevant operation.

The answer was a single platform where each actor has the tools for their part of the lifecycle, and where the connections with processors, manufacturers and accounting systems are explicit and governed. Several reasons shaped it. External parties are treated as integration points, so Emisys stays independent of each third party's internals. APIs are contract-first, so teams can work in parallel against stable interfaces. Authentication and authorization are separate, so identity can change without touching business rules. An outbox with idempotency and at-least-once delivery means a failure never loses or duplicates an operation, and the interface never shows invented data, so users can trust what they see in a financial product. Targets for the goals were deliberately left open until a baseline can be measured after go-live.

</ChallengeSection>

<FeaturesSection title="Features">

The impact map describes five business actors and seven external parties. The external parties, such as a card processor or an accounting system, are modeled only as integration points and never as internal domains.

| Actor | What they can do |
|---|---|
| Fund administrator | Register and update associates, link them to card products, assign cards from batches, activate, block and replace cards, and follow claims on transactions. |
| Associate | See their card, balance and movements, block the card or manage the PIN with stronger identity checks, and open a claim from a specific transaction. |
| Operator in the back-office | Configure funds, limits and a versioned product catalog with approval, manage card batches, and watch an operations dashboard with retries. |
| Reconciliation and accounting analyst | Reconcile periods, manage differences, validate charges and send accounting movements, with period close blocked while exceptions remain. |

### How it works

Emisys is the orchestrator and system of record of a card program run by an employee fund. It is not the switch, the credit authorizer, the reconciliation engine or the ledger, and it never moves money: it calculates, requests, records, evidences and controls. The life of a card goes like this:

1. **Onboarding the fund.** An operator sets up a fund through a checklist with evidence, and the fund goes live only when every mandatory item is met. Products are defined once and then enabled per fund, with overrides.
2. **Registering members.** Associates arrive from the fund's own systems or in bulk, into one canonical model. The fund remains the authority on credit decisions.
3. **Supply.** The fund requests cards, which are grouped into batches and sent to the card processor and manufacturer. Cards return as inventory.
4. **Assignment and delivery.** The fund assigns an available card to a member, which consumes inventory exactly once, and records the physical delivery.
5. **Activation.** The member activates the card in their own app: confirm they hold it, pass a second factor and set the PIN in a secure keypad owned by the processor. Emisys never sees or stores the PIN.
6. **Authorization.** When a purchase arrives, the switch applies its first filter, and Emisys adds context and routes the request to the fund's authorizer within a tight deadline. A timeout is recorded as indeterminate, never as approve or reject.
7. **Transactions and corrections.** Every card operation is one record that accumulates facts: authorization, capture, settlement. Reversals, voids, refunds and chargebacks stay separate and are never merged.
8. **Money control and close.** For each fund and period, Emisys computes the funding and collateral required and records evidence and approvals. It then sends the period's sources to an external reconciliation engine, rates consumption, validates charges, issues the invoice and maps the result to accounting movements.

### How it communicates

Emisys talks to Mottika and to third parties only through versioned, contract-first REST APIs and events. It never reads another system's database. Events are published with an outbox and delivered at least once, and receivers are idempotent. Every operation carries a correlation identifier and a controlled state, and the interface never shows data it does not have: when information from a third party is not available, it says so.

<ImplementationSection title="Implementation & final design">

### Architecture

The project lives in one monorepo with two halves, a Go backend and a web workspace. The architecture follows a C4 design reviewed by an external architecture partner at fixed checkpoints, and changes to it are requested rather than made unilaterally. The backend is hexagonal and sliced by feature: services hold modules per feature and share an internal platform library for configuration, HTTP and gRPC servers, logging, security and safe SQL.

Authentication and authorization are kept apart. Identity is delegated to an OpenID Connect provider, and a separate authorization service answers every permission check over gRPC. If it is unreachable, the answer is a service error and never a silent denial. An API gateway routes requests by path prefix to the right service.

### Domain model

The domain is large, and the core of it can be read in a few ideas. The fund is the root of isolation: nothing is visible across funds, and a fund moves from draft to active and can be suspended and reactivated. An associate belongs to one fund and links to the products enabled for it. A product is versioned, going from editable to authorized, published and suspended, with immutable codes and a change history.

A card stores only a masked number. Its states are assigned, delivered, active and temporarily blocked, with cancelled as a permanent end, and each block has its own origin, so lifting one never lifts another. The authorization operation and the transaction are separate: the first is the request-response moment, the second accumulates the facts of the purchase over time. Settlement, reconciliation and billing are periods and runs with versioned results, so each figure can be explained later. Custom roles by scope, audit records, documents as evidence and integration events cut across all of it.

A few invariants hold everywhere: every critical operation is idempotent, a technical state is never shown as a business state, and no fund can see another's data.

### Quality attributes

The quality attributes were derived from principles and traced to their sources. For availability, the authorization path gets the highest tier, with active-passive cores, multi-zone data, zero-downtime deploys and rebuild drills. For latency, authorization is synchronous with a tight budget and no queues on that path, while long jobs are asynchronous with visible progress. Rate limits per fund keep a noisy fund from hurting the others.

On security, every person uses a second factor, access is deny-by-default, the fund context is resolved on the server, data is encrypted in transit and at rest, and no card number, security code or PIN is stored or logged. For resilience, each third party has timeouts and circuit breakers, retries happen only for transient faults, and an unreachable authority always means access is denied. Every effect, and every denial, is logged so that a critical transaction can be rebuilt end to end, and for interoperability contracts are versioned in one catalog, invalid messages are rejected at the edge and idempotency holds at every boundary.

### UX design

The blueprint defines four surfaces: the back-office, the fund portal, the member app and a governance portal where approvers review settlement and collateral. The member app is phone-first, with one task per screen. The key journeys cover a member activating a card and setting a PIN, a fund onboarding members in bulk and getting a result per row, and a fund requesting a batch, receiving it and assigning cards. They also include a member or fund blocking, cancelling or reissuing a card, an operator handling degraded authorizations with only the permitted recovery actions, and an analyst reconciling a period and approving billing, with four-eyes approval where it matters.

Its principles are one shared design system, the fund context always visible, four kinds of message (success, business rejection, technical failure and "pending confirmation") and sensitive data shown only through short-lived protected components.

### Contracts first

OpenAPI is the specification, linted automatically, with one contract per capability. Contracts are delivered ahead of each build chunk, and a test fails when a contract operation has no handler.

### The web apps

There are three apps by audience, back-office, funds and associates, sharing a design system of shadcn-based primitives with theme tokens. The funds and associates apps are white-label by fund and load each fund's branding after login. The interface uses Space Grotesk and Satoshi, light and dark themes, Spanish and English, and installable PWAs with an offline route. Server-side auth routes in each app handle login, callback, logout, password reset and one-time codes.

### Delivery

Delivery was planned as twenty weeks in five chunks, from operational foundation to reconciliation and billing, with a short refinement and design phase up front and a stabilization phase at the end. Security runs in CI: secret scanning over the full history, static analysis with coverage, and vulnerability scans of the source and of every container image, plus separate deploy workflows for each service and app.

The quality strategy reviews specs before they are built, and testing goes deepest where risk is highest: authorization and its corrections, accounting duplication, isolation between funds and the PIN never being persisted. Integrations are tested at the contract level, and each defect links back to its spec. The repository also includes maps and rules for AI coding agents, with session folders and reviewer checklists, while people keep control of git.

### Decisions

The main decisions, and what each one costs, are summarized below.

| Decision | Why | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| External parties as integration points | Emisys stays independent of each third party's internals. | Every integration needs a contract and an adapter. |
| Contract-first APIs | Teams can work in parallel against stable interfaces. | Contracts must be ready before building starts. |
| Separate authentication and authorization | Identity can change without touching business rules. | One more service to run and call. |
| Outbox, idempotency and at-least-once delivery | A failure never loses or duplicates an operation. | Receivers must be written to tolerate repeats. |
| Never show invented data | Users can trust what they see in a financial product. | Some screens show "unavailable" until a third party answers. |
| Three apps by audience | Each audience gets a focused experience and its own release. | Shared pieces live in a design system, and each app keeps its own types on purpose. |

<ResultsSection title="Results">

Funds and their operators already have the base of the platform: identity and access, funds, products, associates and rules are built, together with the web apps for the back-office, the funds and the associates, with login, password reset, themes, languages and installable PWAs. The next chunks bring card batches and lifecycle, transactions, statements, reconciliation and billing.

The results the client expects are still to be measured: the business targets stay open until a baseline is taken after go-live. The card-processor integration does not exist yet, and legal sign-off and reconciliation formats are among the dependencies on other parties.
